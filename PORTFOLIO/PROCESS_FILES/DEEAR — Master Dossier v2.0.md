## 00. How to Read This Document

This dossier reconstructs DEEAR from the point at which the project actually began: not with a magazine layout, but with a communication problem.

The team wanted to collect personal experiences connected to queer migration, identity, belonging, memory, and emotional transition. The subject matter could be intimate, culturally sensitive, or difficult to articulate. Before any publication could exist, participants first needed conditions in which expression felt possible.

This changes the project’s historical sequence:

```text
Sensitive communication context
→ absence of automatic trust
→ invitation and permission
→ open-ended contribution
→ collection of personal material
→ concept and narrative development
→ editorial system
→ publication
→ later reflection
```

The distinction between **Original Intent** and **Looking Back** is non-negotiable throughout this document.

- **Original Intent** records what was understood, decided, or made during the project.
- **Looking Back** records interpretations that became visible through later reflection.
- A later interpretation may deepen the project, but it must never be presented as though it had been fully articulated at the time.

Reflection is an addition, not a correction.

## 01. Project Definition

### One-sentence positioning

DEEAR is a collaborative editorial project that gathered personal experiences of queer migration through an open, diary-like contribution process and shaped them into a publication designed around intimacy, reflection, and reader participation.

### Expanded definition

DEEAR investigated how an editorial publication could hold experiences that are often fragmented, private, culturally sensitive, or difficult to express in a conventional interview.

The final magazine was not conceived simply as a container for articles. Its diary-like identity, open-ended interview methodology, collection of words and non-verbal materials, reflective pacing, and blank pages were used to create a space in which contributors could share on their own terms and readers could pause, interpret, and respond.

The project’s subject was queer migration. Its deeper communication challenge was how to approach lived experience without forcing it into a single voice, format, or degree of disclosure.

### What the project is

- A collaborative editorial communication project.
- A diary-informed publication about queer migration and lived experience.
- A collection of internal monologues, memories, images, objects, and other personal evidence.
- An experiment in open-ended interviewing and contribution.
- A designed reading environment combining narrative structure, editorial rhythm, and space for reflection.

### What the project is not

- It is not solely a graphic-design or layout exercise.
- It is not a generic LGBTQ+ lifestyle magazine.
- It is not an attempt to speak on behalf of every queer migrant.
- It is not a claim that publication alone creates psychological safety.
- It is not a solo-authored project.
- It is not a retrospective theory disguised as the team’s original brief.

## 03. Historical Context

### Original Intent

The team set out to create a magazine concerned with queer migration. The project considered experiences that could be positive, painful, private, unresolved, or difficult to say aloud.

The team understood that access to those experiences could not be assumed. In some European contexts, queer identity might be discussed with relative openness; in other cultural contexts, particularly those familiar to Kylie in Asia and China, the subject could remain highly private or socially constrained. The project therefore entered what Kylie described as a kind of personal forbidden zone.

The historical problem was practical and ethical:

> How could a group of student designers ask people to share intimate experiences without treating those experiences as content to be extracted?

The team needed source material, but contributors needed agency. They needed to be able to choose:

- whether to respond;
- whether to remain anonymous;
- how much to disclose;
- which medium to use;
- and whether words were the right form at all.

The publication could only begin after this first communication threshold had been crossed.

### Evidence

The project presentation establishes:

- a diary as the conceptual model;
- internal monologue, reflection, and memory as editorial concerns;
- the Five Stages of Grief as an organising reference;
- open-ended contribution rather than a closed questionnaire;
- stories, objects, photographs, sketches, and memories as valid material;
- blank pages as pauses for reader reflection.

Kylie’s invitation establishes:

- explicit anonymity;
- a non-judgemental framing;
- the absence of pressure or obligation;
- permission to ignore the request;
- acceptance of non-verbal forms such as an object, film, song, poem, photograph, drawing, or video;
- a serious and respectful tone designed for first contact.

Kylie’s later account establishes:

- the team consisted of ten people from different countries;
- research recruitment was distributed through each member’s own network;
- Kylie independently created her invitation;
- she used it to reach people outside her immediate circle, including contacts through Weibo and Instagram;
- formality was chosen to communicate seriousness and credibility, not merely as an aesthetic preference.

## 04. The Communication Problem

DEEAR did not begin with the question:

> How should a magazine about queer migration look?

It began with a more fundamental problem:

> How can communication begin when the people holding the knowledge do not yet know or trust the people asking for it?

The initial barrier was not a shortage of possible stories. It was the absence of an established relationship.

For contributors, participation could involve:

- disclosing a private or painful experience;
- speaking across cultural differences;
- explaining something previously left unsaid;
- sharing material that was difficult to translate into an interview answer;
- trusting unfamiliar people with vulnerable information;
- accepting that personal experience might enter a designed publication.

For the team, this created responsibilities:

- not to assume access;
- not to demand a particular form of response;
- not to treat silence as failure;
- not to equate participation with full identification;
- not to frame one person’s account as representative of an entire community.

The project therefore had to design the approach before it could design the archive.

## 05. Trust Building and First Contact

### Original Intent

Kylie needed to contact people with whom no automatic trust already existed. She could not rely on the informality available to teammates who had relevant friends in their immediate circles.

Her solution was a formal invitation.

The invitation was intended to show that:

- the project was serious;
- the request had been considered carefully;
- participation was voluntary;
- anonymity was available;
- contributors could decide what to share;
- no single response format was required;
- declining or ignoring the request was acceptable.

Formality was therefore functional. It communicated intent.

The invitation did not attempt to make disclosure inevitable. It created a more credible and respectful first contact from which a contributor could make an informed choice.

### Communication logic

```text
No existing relationship
→ no automatic trust
→ need to establish credibility
→ need to reduce pressure
→ need to return agency to the participant
→ formal, open invitation
```

### Why this was a design decision

The invitation’s value did not depend primarily on its visual style. Its central design work was the framing of the relationship between requester and participant.

It answered:

- Who is asking?
- Why are they asking?
- How seriously are they treating the subject?
- What can I share?
- Must I use words?
- Can I remain anonymous?
- Can I refuse?

These questions shape whether communication can begin at all.

### Looking Back

Kylie now understands the invitation as a form of **trust design**: a communication device created to make an unfamiliar interaction feel sufficiently credible, bounded, and voluntary.

That term was not necessarily used during the project. It names a function that is visible in the historical decisions and in the invitation itself.

This distinction matters. The historical statement is:

> Kylie created a formal invitation because she needed strangers to understand that the project was serious and that participation would remain voluntary and flexible.

The retrospective interpretation is:

> Looking back, the invitation can be understood as designing the conditions under which trust might begin.

The invitation did not guarantee safety or trust. It signalled care, reduced pressure, and gave potential contributors more control over the first exchange.

## 06. Invitation Strategy

### Individually authored contribution

The invitation was written and designed independently by Kylie.

It emerged from her specific context inside the team:

- she had fewer directly accessible friends with the relevant lived experience;
- she needed to extend the search beyond established relationships;
- she approached friends of friends, social-media contacts, bloggers, and artists;
- the subject could be particularly sensitive for people in Chinese and broader Asian contexts;
- an informal message could appear casual, extractive, or insufficiently credible.

### Strategic decisions embedded in the invitation

#### 1. Seriousness before request

The tone signalled that the project was not a casual attempt to gather interesting stories. This was especially important when approaching strangers.

#### 2. Anonymity as agency

Participants could decide whether their identity should be visible. Anonymity was not treated as an afterthought; it was part of the invitation to participate.

#### 3. Permission not to respond

The invitation explicitly removed obligation. This preserved silence as a valid decision.

#### 4. Multiple forms of expression

Participants were not required to translate experience into polished prose or a conventional interview response. They could contribute:

- an object;
- a film;
- a song;
- a poem;
- a photograph;
- a drawing;
- a video;
- or another form that felt more accurate.

#### 5. Emotional range

The project did not request only inspirational or resolved stories. Positive, painful, private, embarrassing, or difficult experiences could all be relevant.

#### 6. A non-judgemental frame

The language attempted to reduce the fear that a contribution would be assessed, corrected, or morally judged.

### Why the invitation belongs in the archive

The invitation is not peripheral marketing material. It is primary evidence of:

- Kylie’s individual authorship inside a team project;
- the project’s recruitment context;
- the project’s ethical and emotional constraints;
- a decision made before the final visual system existed;
- a communication intervention directed at real potential participants.

It should appear in the project archive before the finished magazine, because it explains how material could enter the project in the first place.

## 07. Interview and Contribution Methodology

### Original Intent

The team did not use a narrowly standardised questionnaire as the only way to participate.

The contribution method was open-ended. It invited stories and memories, but also accepted objects, pictures, sketches, cultural references, and other material capable of carrying personal meaning.

This approach was appropriate because intimate experience does not always arrive as a clear chronological answer. A song, image, object, or fragment may communicate something that a direct question cannot.

### Methodological principles present in the project

#### Participant control

The contributor could control identity, degree of disclosure, and mode of expression.

#### Media flexibility

Words were one option among several, not the universal requirement.

#### Open-ended collection

The project sought an “open-ended collection of their minds,” rather than a dataset normalised into identical responses.

#### Distributed access

Each team member worked outward from their own social and cultural network. This expanded the range of possible participants while making recruitment dependent on different kinds of relationship.

#### Cultural awareness

The project recognised that openness around queer identity varies by context. A method that feels ordinary in one location may feel exposing or unsafe in another.

### Limits

The available records do not establish:

- a formal research-ethics approval process;
- a standard consent form;
- a fully documented anonymisation protocol;
- a fixed interview script used by all ten members;
- the number of participants contacted or included;
- the demographic representativeness of the contribution pool.

The dossier must therefore describe the project as an academic editorial practice with trust-conscious decisions, not as formal social-science research.

## 08. From Collected Material to Concept

### Original Intent

The magazine needed a form capable of holding private experience without reducing it to a list of interviews.

The diary became the central conceptual model because it already carries associations with:

- privacy;
- personal address;
- internal monologue;
- memory;
- confession;
- reflection;
- incomplete thought;
- temporal change.

The diary was therefore not selected only because it offered an attractive visual style. It provided a familiar mental model for entering another person’s interior experience.

### Title logic

“DEEAR” evokes both “Dear…” and “diary.”

This creates an editorial ambiguity:

- a text may be addressed to another person;
- it may be addressed to oneself;
- it may be a private record;
- it may become public through reading;
- the reader may become the next writer.

### Internal monologue

The presentation positioned the project against editorial approaches centred primarily on sex, liberation, or outward expression. DEEAR sought to create space for internal monologue, reflection, and mémoire.

This did not reject public or political dimensions of queer experience. It chose to approach them through interior life.

### Five Stages of Grief

The Five Stages of Grief provided an organising reference for emotional transition.

The source base confirms its presence in the presentation but does not fully document how rigidly each stage governed the final page sequence. Future public copy should therefore describe it as a narrative framework or reference unless the exact chapter-to-stage mapping is verified.

### Looking Back

The diary can now be understood as a communication device that lowered the psychological threshold of participation and reading. It gave the project a pre-existing cultural form associated with privacy and reflection.

That interpretation extends the original design decision; it does not replace it.

## 09. Editorial System

### Editorial purpose

The editorial system translated diverse, personal, and differently formatted contributions into a coherent reading experience without pretending that they were all the same kind of testimony.

The publication needed to balance:

- intimacy and legibility;
- individual voices and collective structure;
- density and pause;
- disclosure and restraint;
- editorial authorship and contributor agency;
- sequence and openness.

### Confirmed editorial devices

#### Diary-like structure

The publication used the diary as both concept and reading cue.

#### Internal-monologue orientation

Material was organised around interior experience rather than only external events.

#### Mixed source material

Words, images, objects, sketches, memories, and other forms could enter the collection.

#### Blank pages

Blank pages were intentionally included so readers could pause, breathe, reflect, or add something of their own.

#### Narrative pacing

The publication used sequence and spacing to move between experiences without turning the magazine into an uninterrupted wall of testimony.

### Blank space as participation

The blank page is one of the clearest places where the magazine moves from content delivery to reader relationship.

It allows:

- a pause after emotionally dense material;
- space for private reflection;
- the possibility of writing back;
- a shift from observing other people’s stories to recognising one’s own position.

It should not be described merely as minimalist styling. Its documented purpose was reflective.

### Visual system

The v1 dossier and project presentation support DEEAR’s identity as an editorial system involving typography, layout, sequence, and publication design. However, exact individual authorship of every page, typographic rule, image treatment, and production specification is not fully established in the retained evidence.

Public material should therefore:

- present the final publication as a team outcome;
- credit Kylie’s participation in graphic and editorial design at the level supported by the original project records;
- avoid implying sole art direction or sole authorship of the complete magazine;
- identify the invitation separately as Kylie’s independent contribution.

## 14. Looking Back

### Trust precedes collection

Looking back, the project’s first designed object was not the magazine. It was the relationship proposed by the invitation.

Before a personal story could become editorial content, a potential contributor needed to understand:

- the seriousness of the request;
- the freedom to decline;
- the possibility of anonymity;
- the openness of the response format;
- and the intended care around the subject.

The invitation did not create trust by itself. It designed signals through which trust might become possible.

### The publication as communication environment

Looking back, the magazine can be understood less as “the communication system” and more as the medium through which a communication environment was assembled.

That environment included:

- contributors;
- the team;
- invitations and first contacts;
- personal material;
- editorial decisions;
- readers;
- pauses;
- and possible responses.

The magazine was the material form that held these relationships together.

### Expression requires conditions

The most important retrospective insight is:

> Before people are willing to communicate, they may first need conditions in which communication feels sufficiently credible, voluntary, and non-judgemental.

This is not a claim that designers can manufacture psychological safety through graphics. It is a more limited and defensible claim: communication design can shape the terms, tone, permissions, and thresholds of an encounter.

### Form as precision

Looking back, DEEAR also helps clarify Kylie’s relationship to form.

Form is not treated as identity or decoration. It determines how accurately an intention can be received.

- The formal invitation communicated seriousness.
- Anonymous participation communicated agency.
- Multiple media communicated openness.
- The diary communicated intimacy and reflection.
- Blank pages communicated pause and participation.
- Editorial sequence communicated relationships between otherwise separate contributions.

Form mattered because each choice changed how the project could be understood and entered.

## 15. Reflection

DEEAR changed the meaning of the project for Kylie over time, but later understanding does not invalidate the earlier work.

At the time, the invitation was a practical and considered response to a real constraint: Kylie lacked an established network of likely contributors and needed to approach unfamiliar people about a sensitive subject.

Looking back, the same decision reveals a broader pattern. When an existing relationship or communication channel is insufficient, Kylie does not necessarily accept that absence as the endpoint. She looks for a form capable of creating better conditions for the exchange.

In DEEAR, that form was an invitation.

The project also clarified that participation is not only the act of speaking. Participation includes control over:

- whether to speak;
- how to speak;
- what to withhold;
- whether to be named;
- and which medium can carry the experience most truthfully.

The final magazine remains important, but it no longer needs to carry the full explanatory burden. The archive now preserves both the published outcome and the earlier communication decisions that made contribution possible.

### Reflection statement

> I originally created the invitation because I needed to approach people who did not already know or trust me. I wanted them to understand that the project was serious, that they could remain anonymous, that they could respond in any form, and that they were under no pressure to participate. Looking back, I recognise that the invitation was doing more than recruiting interviewees. It was shaping the conditions of first contact. DEEAR taught me that communication does not begin when content is published; it begins when another person is given enough clarity, agency, and trust to decide whether they want to enter the exchange.

## 16. Methodology Revealed by the Project

This section is retrospective. It does not claim that the project began with a named methodology.

### 1. Read the context

The topic could not be separated from cultural attitudes, privacy, and the unequal ease with which different people could speak.

### 2. Identify the real barrier

The immediate barrier was not magazine production. It was access without an established relationship.

### 3. Design the first contact

The invitation clarified seriousness, voluntariness, anonymity, and expressive freedom.

### 4. Preserve participant agency

People could decide what to share, how to share it, and whether to take part.

### 5. Accept heterogeneous evidence

Words, images, objects, music, film, poetry, drawing, and memory could all carry experience.

### 6. Translate without flattening

The editorial system needed coherence, but not at the cost of making every voice identical.

### 7. Create room for the reader

Blank pages and pacing made reflection part of the reading experience.

### 8. Revisit without rewriting history

Later reflection reveals additional meaning while keeping the original intention intact.

### Full case-study introduction

DEEAR is a collaborative editorial project about queer migration, but the communication challenge appeared before the first page was designed. The team wanted to gather personal experiences that could be intimate, culturally sensitive, or difficult to express through a conventional interview. Access to those stories could not be assumed.

Working as a graphic designer within the ten-person team, Kylie independently created a formal invitation for her own participant outreach. She needed to approach people beyond her immediate circle, including online contacts who did not already know or trust her. The invitation therefore emphasised seriousness, anonymity, voluntary participation, and freedom of expression. A contribution could be written, but it could also take the form of a song, photograph, object, poem, drawing, film, or another medium.

The collected material was developed into a diary-like publication organised around internal monologue, memory, emotional transition, and reflection. Blank pages created pauses in which readers could process the material or add something of their own. The final magazine was a team outcome; the invitation remains a clearly attributable example of Kylie’s individual communication decision-making.

Looking back, DEEAR reveals that communication does not begin only when content is published. It may begin much earlier, when another person is given enough clarity, agency, and trust to decide whether they want to enter the exchange.

## 26. Canonical Summary

DEEAR was a ten-person collaborative editorial project exploring queer migration through personal experience, internal monologue, memory, and reflection. The team gathered stories and other forms of personal material through distributed networks, then developed them into a diary-like publication using editorial sequence, emotional pacing, and blank pages for reader reflection.

Kylie participated as a graphic designer. Her most clearly documented individual contribution occurred at the project’s beginning: because she needed to approach potential contributors beyond an established personal circle, she independently wrote and designed a formal invitation. The invitation communicated that the project was serious, participation was voluntary, anonymity was possible, and experience could be shared through words, images, objects, songs, poems, drawings, videos, or other media.

Historically, the invitation was Kylie’s practical response to a recruitment and credibility problem. Looking back, it can also be understood as designing the terms of first contact. It did not guarantee trust or safety, but it gave potential contributors greater clarity, agency, and control over whether and how to enter the exchange.

The final magazine remains a collaborative team outcome. Its value lies not only in its editorial design but in the full chain that preceded it:

```text
communication problem
→ trust building
→ invitation strategy
→ open-ended contribution
→ concept development
→ editorial system
→ publication
→ reflection
```

DEEAR’s lasting contribution to Kylie’s practice is the recognition that communication may begin long before a message is published. It begins when the form of an encounter allows another person to decide, with sufficient clarity and agency, whether they are willing to participate.
